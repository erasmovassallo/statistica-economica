8 Production and productivity of economic systems

8.1 The production function

The production function expresses a relationship between input and output: the former represents the factors used to produce (factors of production), the most important of which are labour \(L\) and capital \(K\); the latter the value of the goods and/or services produced (GDP or value added). The advent of technology and of new forms of production processes has in fact excluded other variables, such as land, from the econometric specification, and in other cases has included additional factors, such as the energy required by the process or the education and skills of the workforce. In any case, labour and capital are still the most relevant inputs and often the only ones considered in the statistical and econometric specification of the function, if only because of the difficulty of defining and measuring the other variables.

In a form such as

\[ Y=f(L,K) \tag{8.1} \]

output \(Y\) is usually measured by value added (VA), especially in a macroeconomic approach. For consistency of sources and proper comparability of the data, \(L\) is commonly measured by the number of full-time equivalent labour units (ULA), while \(K\) is measured by gross private fixed capital, given the difficulty of subtracting depreciation (the value of capital consumption) without excessive arbitrariness.

Value added and labour units raise fewer estimation problems, while capital is generally computed indirectly, not without complications, by applying the perpetual inventory method to investment: an estimate of the capital stock at some date in the past is obtained, and investment is added while retirements are subtracted and depreciation is taken into account (at least for the net valuation); but even the valuation of retirements is not simple, being represented by mathematical forms based on somewhat arbitrary assumptions.

The difficulty of estimating private capital is quite evident from the ISTAT time series (among them, those valued at replacement cost are preferable); for the country as a whole, time series cover fewer than 30 years, while territorially disaggregated estimates and estimates for the most recent years are missing.

The problems increase if, following a more complex formulation, one decides to add an estimate of public capital, usually denoted by \(G\) and corresponding in practice to public works. The working assumptions are even stronger in this case: is it right to assume that the value of an investment in a motorway lasts 80 years? How can a secondary road be distinguished from a main road? Should one not account for some wear (more in some places, less in others) of public works? And so on.

Function (8.1) does not satisfy part of the literature, which proposes adding an estimate of public capital \(G\) and an estimate of the capabilities, skills and, in general, abilities of the workforce through a suitable measure of human capital \(H\). The educational level of the employed can be a good proxy for \(H\), on the assumption that higher levels of education (especially technical and scientific training) are strongly correlated with the ability to perform a given job.

The production function might therefore become

\[ Y=f(L,K,G,H) \tag{8.2} \]

The statistical limits in defining and measuring some of the inputs that characterise mathematical forms such as (8.2) go together with non-negligible difficulties in the correct economic interpretation of the estimated coefficients. In short, weighing the pros and cons, it is advisable to use the simpler and more easily interpretable traditional forms, in particular the Cobb-Douglas production function which, moreover, with slight adjustments allows technological change to be taken into account, a rather complex operation in more elaborate mathematical forms. Hence, despite the many criticisms raised, the Cobb-Douglas is still widely applied, mainly because its estimates are easier to interpret.

8.2 The Cobb-Douglas function and returns to scale

In its basic formulation the Cobb-Douglas (CD) is

\[ Y=aL^{\alpha}K^{\beta}\varepsilon \tag{8.3} \]

It is therefore non-linear in the parameters and must be linearised by taking logarithms

\[ \log Y=\log a+\alpha \log L+\beta \log K+\mu \tag{8.4} \]

At this point OLS estimators can be used, provided, as usual, that the statistical conditions for a good regression estimate hold.

The CD is a homogeneous function of degree \(\alpha +\beta\). In particular, if \(\alpha +\beta =1\) we say there are constant returns to scale; that is, if inputs increase by a factor \(k\), output increases proportionally by the same factor. If \(\alpha +\beta <1\), there are decreasing returns to scale: if inputs increase by a scale factor \(k\), output grows less than proportionally. Conversely, if \(\alpha +\beta >1\), output grows more than proportionally to inputs. Clearly \(\alpha +\beta =1\) is a desirable equilibrium condition, but there is generally no economic or statistical reason to assume that this constraint holds a priori.

Under the hypothesis that \(\alpha +\beta =1\), then \(\beta =1-\alpha\) and the functional specification becomes

\[ Y=aL^{\alpha}K^{1-\alpha} \tag{8.5} \]

Dividing both sides by \(L\) we have

\[ Y/L=aL^{\alpha -1}K^{1-\alpha}=a(1/L^{1-\alpha})K^{1-\alpha}=a(K/L)^{\beta} \tag{8.6} \]

that is, the partial productivity of labour \(Y/L\) is a function of capital intensity \(K/L\) through a multiplicative form with exponent \(\beta\).

The \(F\) statistic suggests accepting, at the 95% level, the null hypothesis of constant returns to scale, i.e. \(\alpha +\beta =1\). Moreover, since the estimated coefficient of the regressor in the restricted model is 1.015, a 1% increase in capital intensity leads to an increase of about 1% in labour productivity. This is certainly an anomalous result, since it gives \(\beta\) values close to one and \(\alpha\) values close to zero. In particular, the negative estimate of \(\alpha\) is mainly due to a statistical anomaly for a coefficient here considered close to zero.

8.3 Elasticities, marginal productivities and Euler’s theorem

Recalling that

\[ \partial Y/\partial L=a\alpha L^{\alpha -1}K^{1-\alpha} \tag{8.7} \]

and since the elasticity \(e\) is the ratio of two relative changes, which in the case of labour are \(\Delta Y/Y\) and \(\Delta L/L\), we have

\[ e=(\Delta Y/Y)/(\Delta L/L) \tag{8.8} \]

and, in continuous terms,

\[ e=(\partial Y/\partial L)/(Y/L)=a\alpha L^{\alpha -1}K^{1-\alpha}/a(K/L)^{1-\alpha}=\alpha \tag{8.9} \]

so the elasticity of \(Y\) with respect to \(L\) (i.e. \(e_{L}\)) is exactly \(\alpha\). Similarly, the elasticity of \(Y\) with respect to capital \(K\) (\(e_{K}\)) is \(\beta =1-\alpha\). Therefore, if \(L\) increases by \(k\%\), \(Y\) increases by \(k\alpha \%\), and if \(K\) increases by \(k\%\), \(Y\) increases by \(k(1-\alpha )\%\).

Hence, if

\[ e_{L}=(\partial Y/\partial L)/(Y/L)=\alpha \qquad \text{and} \qquad e_{K}=(\partial Y/\partial K)/(Y/K)=\beta \tag{8.10} \]

then also

\[ \partial Y/\partial L=\alpha (Y/L) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \partial Y/\partial K=\beta (Y/K)=(1-\alpha )(Y/K) \tag{8.11} \]

Consequently, the marginal productivity of labour is \(\alpha\)-proportional to the average productivity of labour, while the marginal productivity of capital is \(\beta\)-proportional to the average productivity of capital.

Indeed, with the data for Mexico we obtain approximately (in the restricted model the estimated coefficient is \(\beta\), hence \(\alpha =1-\beta\))

Note the unusual negative value for the labour factor.

Following marginalist theory under perfect competition, the productivity of a factor must equal its relative price

\[ \partial Y/\partial L=P_{L}=\alpha (Y/L) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \partial Y/\partial K=P_{K}=(1-\alpha )(Y/K) \tag{8.14} \]

It follows that

\[ \begin{gathered}Y=P_{L}L+P_{K}K=\alpha (Y/L)L+(1-\alpha )(Y/K)K= \\ \alpha Y+(1-\alpha )Y=Y\end{gathered} \tag{8.15} \]

that is, \(\alpha\) is also the share of \(Y\) attributable to labour and \((1-\alpha)\) the share attributable to capital.

If \(Y=aL^{\alpha}K^{\beta}\) with \(\log Y=\log a+\alpha \log L+\beta \log K\), defining \(\nabla Z=Z_{t}-Z_{t-1}\) for any variable \(Z\) at each time \(t\), it follows that

\[ \nabla \log Y=\alpha \nabla \log L+\beta \nabla \log K \tag{8.16} \]

Since \(\nabla \log\) is the growth rate, we conclude that the growth rate of output \(Y\) is approximately the weighted sum of the growth rates of the inputs labour \(L\) and capital \(K\). We obtain

The implicit assumptions, legitimate for adjacent periods, are the stability of the coefficients \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) and an unchanged technology, which become untenable over long periods.

Therefore, to account explicitly for the effect of technology, one can use Solow’s well-known formulation, in which technical progress is computed as a residual. We can write (the usual conditions of neoclassical theory hold, with \(\alpha +\beta =1\))

\[ Y=A(t)L^{\alpha}K^{\beta} \tag{8.17} \]

where \(A(t)\) represents the effect of technical progress.

Hence \(\log Y=\log A(t)+\alpha \log L+\beta \log K\) and, differencing over periods, \(\nabla \log Y=\nabla \log A(t)+\alpha \nabla \log L+\beta \nabla \log K\), which gives the Solow residual

\[ \nabla \log A(t)=\nabla \log Y-(\alpha \nabla \log L+\beta \nabla \log K) \tag{8.18} \]

It is also interesting to note that \(Y=A(t)L^{\alpha}K^{\beta}\) can be rewritten as

\[ A(t)=Y/L^{\alpha}K^{\beta}=TFP \tag{8.19} \]

In this formulation \(A(t)\) is not linked to the Solow residual but to a measure of total factor productivity (TFP), since it is the ratio of output \(Y\) to a combination of the two factors of production through a geometric mean with weights \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\). Thus an index of how TFP changes over time (or space) is simply a total productivity index, given by the ratio of two TFPs for different periods (or areas). In essence, this is a non-parametric method with strict assumptions: perfectly competitive markets, constant returns to scale, Hicks-neutral technical progress (i.e. technical progress changes the marginal productivity of the factors in the same proportion), and no adjustment costs (so firms always tend towards a long-run equilibrium).

8.5 Technical progress in the Cobb-Douglas

The functional specification of the Cobb-Douglas can be enriched by an additional variable expressing technical progress acting over time; in this way one could capture improvements in input quality over time, such as a progressively better-qualified workforce or machinery able to produce faster and with less waste. In this case we could write

\[ Y=aL^{\alpha}K^{\beta}e^{ut} \tag{8.20} \]

Clearly, if \(\alpha +\beta =1\) were imposed when it does not hold, the term \(e^{ut}\) would also absorb the effects of returns to scale.

Finally, note that in the Cobb-Douglas specification with technical progress, changes in labour quality can also be considered explicitly through a measure of hours worked. For example

\[ Y=aL^{\alpha}K^{\beta}h^{T}e^{ut} \tag{8.21} \]

where \(h\) is the average number of hours worked per employee.

8.6 More general forms: CES, VES and translog

To conclude, it is worth mentioning production functions that are more general than the CD, but also less used in empirical analysis because they are harder to interpret and to estimate statistically. In particular, the Cobb-Douglas has a constant elasticity of substitution (between \(L\) and \(K\)) equal to one; this assumption is not always admissible. There are more complex forms that remove some of these constraints, for example functions with a constant elasticity of substitution not necessarily equal to one, the so-called CES functions. VES functions, similarly, generalise to the case of a non-constant elasticity of substitution; among these forms the best known is the translog

\[ \begin{gathered}\log Y=b_{0}+b_{1}\log L+b_{2}\log K+b_{3}(\log L)^{2}+ \\ b_{4}(\log K)^{2}+b_{5}(\log L\log K)\end{gathered} \tag{8.22} \]
