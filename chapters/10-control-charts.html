10 Statistical control charts for quality analysis

10.1 Control charts and the 3-sigma method

The pursuit of higher quality in production processes, understood as adherence to increasingly demanding standards, has characterised the world’s economies since the 1950s. From the Japanese experience came a theory of total quality aimed at the control and continuous improvement of the entire business process, towards an overall optimisation of the system. An important aspect of the pursuit of higher quality is knowledge of the process and its control, so that it meets certain conditions that translate into specific statistical criteria; for example, the description of the variability shown by the process and the possible presence of abnormal fluctuations are particularly important. For this purpose, control charts are a tool that is both very useful and easy to read.

Control charts, originally proposed by Shewhart, are simply graphs showing the evolution over time of the production process, i.e. of the output or of some of its characteristics. For example, if we are monitoring the number of defective screws produced by a machine over some periods of time, we do not want a systematic increase in defects, or in any case one that would make the analyst worry about the need to recalibrate or even repair the machine; but if defects are too numerous, the control chart will signal the anomaly with fluctuations of the process beyond specific limits, usually set at \(\pm 3\sigma\) from the mean of the process itself.

In this regard, Figure 10.1 shows made-up data on the breaking strength of some nails, measured daily. The process follows an irregular pattern, increasing up to the 13th observation, but still within the acceptance limits (the dashed horizontal lines).

The subsequent observations jump upwards and signal an out-of-control situation, which however returns within the limits after a few days; from then on the process drifts progressively until it crosses the lower limit at the 32nd observation. So even a simple graph of some characteristic of the good or service under study can be an excellent analytical tool.

More precisely, if \(\mu\) is the mean of the process, we can define two control limits following the so-called 3-sigma method:

\[ \text{lower control limit } LCL=\mu -3\sigma \qquad \text{and} \qquad \text{upper control limit } UCL=\mu +3\sigma \tag{10.1} \]

Note that if the data follow a normal distribution, the interval \(\mu \pm 3\sigma\) includes more than 99% of the observations, exactly 99.73%, so the probability of a type I error is 0.0027. If we wanted to reason in probabilistic terms and set a priori a type I error probability of 0.001 (i.e. 0.1%) for each limit, the multiplier of the standard deviation would be 3.09

Note that, to promptly signal anomalous patterns that might lead to an out-of-control situation beyond the traditional 3-sigma limit, 2-sigma warning limits can be added, i.e. a type I error of 0.025 can be set. The application of control charts is also very interesting for public services and for the analysis of the quality perceived by the users of a service, for example health care.

10.2 The p chart

A well-known control chart is the \(p\) chart for attributes, used for products that can be classified as defective or non-defective with respect to some characteristic. This does not apply only to industry: in health care, for example, one may consider whether certain thresholds for the cleanliness of premises are exceeded, and so on. The chart analyses the proportion of defective items \(p\), i.e. the share of items that do not conform to specific company standards. The link with the binomial distribution, which has two possible states, is immediate. So, if the sample of items inspected has size \(n\) and \(D\) is the number of nonconforming units, we have \(P(D=x)=\binom{n}{x}p^{x}(1-p)^{n-x}\), \(x=0,1,...,n\). The share of nonconforming items is \(\hat{p}=D/n\), hence \(\mu_{\hat{p}}=p\) and \(\sigma_{\hat{p}}=(p(1-p)/n)^{1/2}\). Consequently, if the true fraction of nonconforming items is known, we write

\[ LCL=p-3(p(1-p)/n)^{1/2} \qquad \text{and} \qquad UCL=p+3(p(1-p)/n)^{1/2} \tag{10.2} \]

Of course, the samples inspected are usually \(m>1\), not always of the same size \(n\) and, above all, the true fraction \(p\) is almost never known; instead, it is estimated from the sample data as \(\bar{p}=\sum_{i} D_{i}/\sum_{i} n_{i}\), giving

\[ LCL=\bar{p}-3(\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})/\bar{n})^{1/2} \qquad \text{and} \qquad UCL=\bar{p}+3(\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})/\bar{n})^{1/2} \tag{10.3} \]

where \(\bar{p}\) is the average proportion of defective items and \(\bar{n}\) the average sample size.

def carta(valori, lcl, ucl, titolo, ylim, ylabel=""):
    """Draws a control chart with constant (scalar) or variable (vector) limits."""
    t = np.arange(1, len(valori) + 1)
    plt.figure(figsize=(5, 5))
    plt.plot(t, valori, "o-", color="black", markerfacecolor="none")
    for lim in (lcl, ucl):
        if np.ndim(lim) == 0:
            plt.axhline(lim, color="black", linestyle="--", linewidth=0.8)
        else:
            plt.plot(t, lim, color="black", linestyle="--", linewidth=0.8)
    plt.ylim(*ylim); plt.title(titolo); plt.xlabel("Index"); plt.ylabel(ylabel)
    plt.show()

In this case the chart is built for samples of constant size, assuming that the number of defects \(X\) follows a Poisson random variable with parameter \(c\) (average number of defects), \(P(X=x)=e^{-c}c^{x}/x!\), \(x=0,1,2,...\), hence with limits

\[ LCL=c-3c^{1/2} \qquad \text{and} \qquad UCL=c+3c^{1/2} \tag{10.4} \]

But if the number of defects, and hence the average number \(c\), refers to several production units, say \(n\), it may be useful to build the chart around the average number of nonconformities per unit, i.e. on \(u=c/n\), with limits \(LCL=u-3(u/n)^{1/2}\) and \(UCL=u+3(u/n)^{1/2}\), thus obtaining the \(u\) chart.

10.5 Control charts for variables: the X-bar and R charts

Control charts for attributes are typically applied to categorical data on the presence or absence of a defect. Conversely, control charts for variables deal with quantitative data on a characteristic \(X\) of the product, such as weight, volume, etc. In this case it is advisable to keep the process under control both for the mean and for the variability; indeed, control charts for variables are applied in pairs.

If the characteristic is such that \(X\sim N(\mu ,\sigma^{2})\), the sample mean is \(\bar{X}\sim N(\mu ,\sigma_{\bar{X}}^{2})\), where \(\sigma_{\bar{X}}=\sigma /n^{1/2}\). The 3-sigma control chart for the mean then has limits

\[ LCL=\mu -3\sigma_{\bar{X}} \qquad \text{and} \qquad UCL=\mu +3\sigma_{\bar{X}} \tag{10.5} \]

and, in probability terms, \(\mu -Z_{\alpha /2}\sigma_{\bar{X}}\) and \(\mu +Z_{\alpha /2}\sigma_{\bar{X}}\).

Generally \(\mu\) is unknown and must be estimated by the mean of the sample means \(\bar{\bar{X}}\); similarly, the sample standard deviation is used for \(\sigma\).

To analyse variability, as an alternative to the standard deviation, one can use the range \(R\), i.e. the mean \(\bar{R}\) of the sample ranges. The following limits are thus defined for the \(\bar{X}\) chart (centre line \(\bar{\bar{X}}\))

\[ LCL=\bar{\bar{X}}-A_{2}\bar{R} \qquad \text{and} \qquad UCL=\bar{\bar{X}}+A_{2}\bar{R} \tag{10.6} \]

and those for the \(R\) chart (centre line \(\bar{R}\))

\[ LCL=D_{3}\bar{R} \qquad \text{and} \qquad UCL=D_{4}\bar{R} \tag{10.7} \]

Note the use of the specific constants \(A_{2}\), \(D_{3}\) and \(D_{4}\), given in dedicated tables.

If the characteristic under examination follows a normal distribution, there is a specific relationship between the range and the standard deviation. If \(d_{2}\) is the mean and \(d_{3}\) the standard deviation of the random variable \(R/\sigma\), an estimate of \(\sigma\) is \(\hat{\sigma}=\bar{R}/d_{2}\). Since the limits of the chart for the mean can be defined as \(\bar{\bar{X}}\pm 3\hat{\sigma}/n^{1/2}\), and hence as \(\bar{\bar{X}}\pm 3\bar{R}/(d_{2}n^{1/2})\), we immediately obtain \(A_{2}=3/(d_{2}n^{1/2})\).

Similarly, we can write \(\sigma_{R/\sigma}=\sigma_{R}/\sigma\), \(\hat{\sigma}_{R}=\hat{\sigma}d_{3}\), \(\hat{\sigma}_{R}=\bar{R}d_{3}/d_{2}\), \(\bar{R}+3\hat{\sigma}_{R}=(1+3d_{3}/d_{2})\bar{R}\), so that \(D_{4}=1+3d_{3}/d_{2}\); likewise, \(D_{3}=1-3d_{3}/d_{2}\) (set to 0 when negative).

It should be stressed that the chart for the mean measures the variability between samples, while the range chart measures the variability within samples. As the formulas also show, this implies that \(\sigma\) must be estimated using only the information on within-sample variability, so the usual estimator of the standard deviation computed on all the data must not be used.

10.6 The X-bar and S charts

The \(R\) chart is preferable for small samples, but for sizes above 5 the estimate of \(\sigma\) based on the range becomes progressively less precise. Generally, with \(n>10\) or with variable sample sizes, a specific \(S\) or \(S^{2}\) chart is preferred to the \(R\) chart; of course the construction of the corresponding \(\bar{X}\) chart is also affected.

Let us assume, as usual, that the characteristic \(X\) of the product follows a normal distribution. If \(\sigma\) is known, the \(S\) chart has centre line \(c_{4}\sigma\), with lower and upper 3-sigma limits equal to

\[ LCL=B_{5}\sigma \qquad \text{and} \qquad UCL=B_{6}\sigma \tag{10.8} \]

where the constants \(c_{4}\), \(B_{5}\) and \(B_{6}\) take specific values tabulated by sample size, such that \(B_{5}=c_{4}-3(1-c_{4}^{2})^{1/2}\) and \(B_{6}=c_{4}+3(1-c_{4}^{2})^{1/2}\).

If the standard deviation is unknown, it must be estimated using the relationship \(\hat{\sigma}=\bar{S}/c_{4}\), where \(\bar{S}\) is the mean of the sample standard deviations. The \(S\) chart then has centre line \(\bar{S}\), with limits

\[ LCL=B_{3}\bar{S} \qquad \text{and} \qquad UCL=B_{4}\bar{S} \tag{10.9} \]

where \(B_{3}=1-(3/c_{4})(1-c_{4}^{2})^{1/2}\) and \(B_{4}=1+(3/c_{4})(1-c_{4}^{2})^{1/2}\).

The limits of the \(\bar{X}\) chart (whose centre line is still \(\bar{\bar{X}}\)) must then also be redefined

\[ LCL=\bar{\bar{X}}-A_{3}\bar{S} \qquad \text{and} \qquad UCL=\bar{\bar{X}}+A_{3}\bar{S} \tag{10.10} \]

where \(A_{3}=3/(c_{4}n^{1/2})\).

Building the \(\bar{X}\) and \(S\) charts is straightforward even with variable sample sizes. The centre lines are obtained using suitable weighted means instead of simple means, while the control limits, which are also variable, are computed each time with the constants for the corresponding sample size. Finally, note that some prefer the \(S^{2}\) chart, based on the sample variance, to the \(S\) chart.

In addition to the classical Shewhart charts presented here, the literature has proposed many other techniques, generally more detailed and more sensitive to small shifts in the process, such as CUSUM (cumulative sum) and EWMA (exponentially weighted moving average) charts; we will not dwell on them further.
