4 The decomposition approach to time series

4.1 The classical approach and the components of a series

A set of data ordered in time defines a time series. For example, Italian GDP between 2000 and 2008 is a time series which, in this case, spans 8 periods and 9 years. Knowing how and why phenomena evolve over time allows us to formulate plausible hypotheses about their future evolution and to take corrective action where needed.

There are essentially two approaches to time-series analysis: the classical and the modern one (leaving aside spectral analysis). The first developed historically around empirical considerations, with techniques grounded in the analyst’s observation and experience. The idea behind the classical approach is that the series can be decomposed into a few specific factors. The modern approach, by contrast, assumes that there is a data-generating mechanism, of which the observed time series is one of many possible realisations; if certain initial assumptions hold, it is possible to find the statistical model that adequately represents the data. We will return to the modern approach later.

As we said, the classical approach decomposes the series into a few factors, namely

\(Y\) \(T\) \(C\) \(S\) \(A\)
series trend cycle seasonality random residual

The trend \(T\) is the most important component, since it represents the underlying tendency of the phenomenon. The cycle \(C\) expresses wave-like fluctuations over the medium term; in economics it can be represented by phases of recession and expansion, but it cannot always be clearly distinguished from the trend, so much so that trend and cycle are often treated as a single trend-cycle component \(TC\). Seasonality \(S\) is present only if the series consists of sub-annual observations, and expresses the typical seasonal pattern of the phenomenon. Finally, the random component \(A\) (sometimes denoted \(I\)) is a purely random residual factor, due to chance or to non-systematic errors.

These factors are combined through additive, multiplicative or mixed models (or other forms), respectively

\[ \begin{gathered}Y=T+C+S+A \\ Y=T\cdot C\cdot S\cdot A \\ Y=T\cdot C+S+A\end{gathered} \tag{4.1} \]

The additive model is simpler to compute but assumes a less plausible logical independence among the component factors; in this sense, the multiplicative model often appears the most appropriate for economic time series. Note also that in the multiplicative model, unlike the additive one, only the trend (or trend-cycle) component has the same unit of measurement as the series \(Y\), while the other components take the form of dimensionless coefficients; this makes the multiplicative model easy to read.

To bring out the underlying tendency of the series we must reduce the interference of short- and medium-term fluctuations. One way of doing this is to filter the data through, for example, an average computed over a number of adjacent values, that is, a “moving average” such as

\[ MA_{i}=(x_{i-1}+x_{i}+x_{i+1})/3 \qquad \text{for 3 terms} \tag{4.2} \]

An odd-order moving average is always centred, in the sense that the resulting value corresponds to the central term among those involved, with the same number of terms on either side. Clearly, a moving average cannot be computed at the lower and upper ends of the series, for lack of information outside the observed range: with 3 terms the first and last values are lost, but with 5 terms instead of 3, two values will be missing on the left and two on the right. In general, if \(L\) is the number of adjacent terms in the average, we lose \((L-1)/2\) terms at both the beginning and the end of the sequence. A larger number of terms smooths the series more, making the underlying tendency clearer, but loses more data at the beginning and end of the period under analysis.

Moving averages of even order can of course also be used, with the drawback that they are not centred to begin with; we will come back to this shortly.

To apply a moving average to the data of Figure 4.1 we use the filter() function. 4.3 Exponential smoothing

Another way of smoothing a series in search of long-run patterns is exponential smoothing. The technique is particularly interesting because, unlike the previous one, it allows short-term forecasts. The simplest model is best applied to data with no seasonality and no particularly marked trend. Exponential smoothing is nothing but an exponentially weighted moving average

\[ M_{t}=\alpha x_{t}+(1-\alpha )M_{t-1} \tag{4.3} \]

where \(0<\alpha <1\) and \(M_{1}=x_{1}\), and

  • \(x_{t}\) is the value of the time series in period \(t\)
  • \(M_{t}\) is the weighted average in period \(t\)
  • \(M_{t-1}\) is the weighted average in period \(t-1\)
  • \(\alpha\) is the weighting coefficient, or smoothing parameter, chosen a priori.

A low \(\alpha\) filters out random fluctuations well, while a high \(\alpha\) adapts quickly to systematic changes in the series. A value of \(\alpha\) close to 0 gives strong smoothing, while a value close to 1 emphasises short-run fluctuations; the forecast for the first period after the observed values is set equal to the last smoothed value

\[ F_{t+1}=\hat{x}_{t+1}=M_{t} \tag{4.4} \]

The technique can be applied recursively to produce forecasts over several periods, but the probability of a wrong forecast then grows steadily.

Following the G.M. sales example, with \(\alpha = 0.25\) we have

4.5 Non-linear trends

Since we want to identify the long-run direction of movement without worrying too much about the fit to the data, the trend of a series can generally be represented by a straight line; over the medium to long run, however, it is reasonable to look for non-linear trends too. Looking for a non-linear trend becomes necessary when trend and cycle are not treated as separate components, so that the (non-seasonal) fluctuation of the series must be captured by the estimated model. There is also a strong motivation when looking for a long-run trend: for example, if over 30 years the trend had changed direction halfway through, a linear model might fail to capture the current direction and to forecast the future direction of the series correctly; in such conditions, a non-linear trend would help draw more reliable conclusions.

We load data on Kodak’s revenues for 1975-1998, contained in the file entrate.csv. We drop the assumption of a linear trend and now also look for a quadratic one. Clearly the exponential-trend hypothesis is not realistic for this series.

If, for interpretive purposes, both a linear and a non-linear trend seem plausible, a simple way of choosing among linear, quadratic and exponential trends is based on first, second and relative differences. Essentially, a linear trend fits the series well if the first differences are roughly constant; the same holds for second differences and the quadratic trend, while an exponential trend goes with constant relative differences.

Returning to our example, none of the three kinds of differences is especially helpful in suggesting one form of trend over another, although the quadratic trend immediately looks more interesting since it captures the decline in the series. A deterministic method of this kind is clearly not very efficient. If one nevertheless wanted to find the best trend, considering all three forms plausible, one could analyse the OLS estimates of the coefficients and the goodness-of-fit statistics of the corresponding models.

4.8 The seasonal component

We continue our treatment of the classical approach to time series by analysing phenomena with a seasonal component. For ease of interpretation we choose a multiplicative model.

Given the time series \(Y\), we want to identify its components. A moving average allows us to identify the trend, or rather the trend-cycle, but there is no unique answer to the choice of its order. A 3-term moving average has the advantage of losing only one observation at the beginning and one at the end, while a higher order smooths the data better but loses more observations. The choice of order depends largely on the desired degree of smoothing, but also on how many observations are available.

If a seasonal component is present, the order of the moving average must take the seasonal period into account, to make sure that the filter, matching the length of the season, cancels the seasonal component without distorting the trend-cycle. So the order of the average will be 12 for monthly data, 4 for quarterly data, 2 for half-yearly data, and so on; and since the number of terms is generally even, the moving average must also be centred, to ensure that its value can be unambiguously attributed to the time at the centre of the average. Consequently, with a 4-term moving average, centring amounts to taking the arithmetic mean of two adjacent 4-term moving averages, that is, a weighted average of 5 terms in which the first and last receive a weight of 0.5 (so in the end two values will be missing at the head and two at the tail of the moving-average series)

\[ MM(4)_{C}=(0.5\cdot x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{4}+0.5\cdot x_{5})/4 \tag{4.6} \]

Using a moving average whose order equals the length of the seasonal period has an intuitive justification since, as we said, the series must be filtered so as to remove the seasonal effect without introducing distortions, as a moving average of a different order would: with quarterly data, for example, a 3-term average would always leave out some quarter, while a 5-term one would include one quarter twice.

Having obtained the trend or trend-cycle, since \(Y=T\cdot C\cdot S\cdot A\), the gross seasonal component (including the irregular component) is easily determined

\[ SA=Y/(T\cdot C) \tag{4.7} \]

Note straight away that, by construction, \(SA\) is an index number that fluctuates around one and has no unit of measurement. In an additive model little changes, except that we subtract instead of dividing

\[ SA=Y-(T + C) \tag{4.8} \]

In this case \(SA\) has the same unit of measurement as \(Y\), so the preference for a multiplicative model simplifies this point too.

The seasonal component obtained in this way is variable, since the values change slightly between the same periods of different years, whereas large fluctuations would be quite counter-intuitive: there is no strong reason why, especially in the short run, the season should change markedly from one year to the next; if it does, the change can hardly be attributed to seasonal factors as such. In practice, seasonal fluctuations are often numerically quite small relative to the average level of the series, and very similar from one year to the next; so it is not misleading to treat seasonality as a phenomenon that evolves at a constant rate. Thus, even for very long seasonal series, it is convenient and reasonable to represent the seasonal pattern by single values, for example 12 values for monthly seasonality, 4 for quarters, 2 for half-years, etc., simply by averaging within the same seasonal period.

This simplification consists in averaging the same seasonal factor year after year: with monthly data, for example, we average the seasonal factors for January over all the years of the series, then do the same for February and so on up to December, obtaining 12 single values (one per month) that replace the individual seasonal factors making up these averages. It may happen, however, simply because of numerical approximation, that the sum of these new constant seasonal coefficients is not exactly 12 (with a multiplicative model, or 0 with an additive one), as it should be, since they represent a seasonal phenomenon that begins and ends within the year. As seasonality must cancel out within the year, the seasonal factors below one (below 0 in the additive model) must be offset by those above one (above 0 in the additive model), so their sum must equal 4 for quarters, 3 for four-month periods, 12 for months, etc., or 0 in the additive model. This may fail to happen because of numerical approximation; so, assuming the hypothesis of constant seasonality is sound, a correction based on proportionality can be applied.

Specifically, consider a quarterly series covering 3 years; the table below shows the seasonal factors obtained from the ratio \(Y/(T\cdot C)\), where the trend-cycle is a centred 4-term moving average. The first two and the last two values are of course missing:

seasonal factor 1 2 3 4
year 1 – – a b
year 2 c d e f
year 3 g h – –
mean \(m_1\) \(m_2\) \(m_3\) \(m_4\)

with \(m_1 + m_2 + m_3 + m_4 = somma\) (the sum). We compute the mean of each column; the sum of the means should be exactly 4, the seasonal period, apart from small fluctuations: for example, it is reasonable to get 4.01 or 3.90 (to keep the explanation intuitive), but certainly not 2 or 7, values far from 4; in fact the sum is never far from its theoretical value. At this point, the difference between the theoretical 4 and the value actually obtained is spread over the 4 quarters proportionally

\[ m_{i}\cdot 4/somma \qquad \text{for each } i \text{ from 1 to 4} \tag{4.9} \]

This gives the “corrected” seasonal indices, identical from year to year, whose sum is now, by construction, exactly 4. The procedure applies to any other seasonal period.

4.10 Seasonality with dummy variables

Besides trend analysis, regression can be used to isolate the seasonal component through dummy variables added to the explanatory variables and used as seasonal labels. With monthly data we add 11 dummy variables, taking the value 1 for observations in the month they represent and 0 otherwise; the twelfth dummy is not included, to avoid statistical redundancy, since its effect is already absorbed by the constant of the model. Which dummy to exclude is entirely arbitrary and does not affect the conclusions; we will return to this point in later sections.

Keeping to the example of monthly data, we estimate a model of the form

\[ Y=a+bt+c_{1}D_{1}+c_{2}D_{2}+...+c_{10}D_{10}+c_{11}D_{11} \tag{4.11} \]

where the twelfth dummy, for December, has been omitted; the seasonal component here therefore measures the impact of each month relative to December as the reference. \(a+bt\) is the trend or trend-cycle (in the latter case a polynomial of degree higher than one is usually assumed), while \(c_{1}D_{1}+c_{2}D_{2}+...+c_{10}D_{10}+c_{11}D_{11}\) is the seasonal component, with \(D_{i}=1\) in the \(i\)-th month and 0 otherwise. Note that the model implicitly imposes a constant seasonal structure.

4.11 Autoregressive models

By their nature, the models discussed so far do not allow reliable forecasts except under particularly strict conditions, and in any case only in the short run. Dealing satisfactorily with forecasting requires more refined estimation methods and models, moving for example from the deterministic approach of many classical methods to a stochastic one, typical of ARIMA models. We will return to this later, but it is worth mentioning here a special case, useful for non-seasonal series not subject to wide and frequent fluctuations: the autoregressive model, which explains the series \(Y\) through the same variable lagged in time

\[ Y_{t}=a_{0}+a_{1}Y_{t-1}+...+a_{p}Y_{t-p}+e_{t} \tag{4.13} \]

that is, an autoregressive model of order \(p\), \(AR(p)\).

Note that estimating these models by OLS, as for linear models in general, requires certain conditions that must be checked. Given the autoregressive nature of the model, one can sense that least-squares estimates for specifications of order higher than one are inadequate; we will come back to this later.

4.12 Distributed-lag models

In the autoregressive model of order \(p\), \(Y\) is explained by itself lagged up to period \(t-p\), but a more complex model might also include other explanatory variables \(X\), lagged or not, for example

\[ Y_{t}=\alpha +\beta_{1}Y_{t-1}+\beta_{2}Y_{t-2}+\gamma_{0}X_{t}+\gamma_{1}X_{t-1} \tag{4.14} \]

In the simplest case only \(X\), lagged for some periods, might be present; we then have a distributed-lag model of the form

\[ Y_{t}=\alpha +\gamma_{0}X_{t}+...+\gamma_{k}X_{t-k}+...+\gamma_{K}X_{t-K} \tag{4.15} \]

with \(k=0,...,K\).

4.16 The Holt-Winters method

Finally, R also offers built-in functions for exponential smoothing. The smoothing model presented earlier is rather simple and should not be used for forecasting series with a marked trend and/or seasonal fluctuations. In such cases the Holt-Winters procedure is preferable; with a multiplicative seasonal component, the forecast \(F\) at time \(t+Q\) becomes

\[ F_{t+Q}=(a_{t}+b_{t}Q)S_{t-L+Q} \tag{4.17} \]

with

\[ \begin{aligned} a_{t} &= M_{t}=\alpha (x_{t}/S_{t-L})+(1-\alpha )(M_{t-1}+b_{t-1}) \\ b_{t} &= \beta (M_{t}-M_{t-1})+(1-\beta )b_{t-1} \\ S_{t} &= \gamma (x_{t}/M_{t})+(1-\gamma )S_{t-L} \end{aligned} \]

and \(0<\alpha <1\), \(0<\beta <1\), \(0<\gamma <1\), where \(L\) is the length of the seasonal period (4 for quarters, 12 for months, etc.); additive seasonality can also be assumed, with some changes to the formulas not reported here.
