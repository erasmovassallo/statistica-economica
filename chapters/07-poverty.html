7 Poverty and income distribution

7.1 Relative and absolute poverty

Any definition of poverty involves a degree of arbitrariness on the part of the researcher. The most important definitions refer to a concept of relative poverty and to one of absolute poverty. In the first case a poverty threshold is chosen that depends on the level of well-being of the community; for example, if a family of two adults without children has a level of (net) disposable income, or a level of consumption expenditure, below the average of Italian families, it will be said to be (relatively) poor. A family that is poor in absolute terms, instead, cannot afford a specific basket of essential goods and/or services. Between absolute and relative poverty there is a variety of intermediate definitions, whose relevance is, however, more theoretical than empirical. Not by chance, in the most industrialised countries the notion of relative poverty is the one most widely adopted, also for policy purposes. Conversely, in developing countries absolute poverty is the main reference, since the interest generally lies in food-support policies for large sections of the population.

From a historical perspective, ISTAT data for 2007 show about 2 million 653 thousand families in relative poverty, 11% of resident families in 2007, while poor individuals numbered 7 million 542 thousand, 13% of the whole population.

The percentage of poor families and persons out of all resident families and persons is generally estimated on the basis of a conventional threshold (poverty line), which identifies the level of consumption expenditure below which a family is defined as poor in relative terms. The poverty threshold for a family of two adults (the standard reference) is the average monthly expenditure per person, which in 2007 was 986.35 euro (1.6% more than in 2006). Two-person families whose average monthly expenditure is equal to or below this value are classified as relatively poor. For families of different size (and composition) the poverty line is obtained by applying an equivalence scale that accounts for some economies of scale; for example, spending on lighting or heating depends little on the number of members compared with food expenditure (Table 7.1).

7.3 Poverty lines and equivalence scales

We have seen that in Italy the standard poverty line for 2007 was 986.35 euro. Computing poverty lines is generally rather complex, since it requires formulations based on demand systems such as QUAIDS (Quadratic Almost Ideal Demand System). For descriptive purposes, here we refer instead to a simple traditional approach based on Engel expenditure functions such that

\[ A/S=a+b\log S+c\log N \tag{7.1} \]

where \(A\) is food expenditure, \(S\) total expenditure and \(N\) the number of family members.

Other relevant information, such as age, social characteristics or consumer preferences, is ignored. Assuming that the ratio \(A/S\) stays constant and setting \(q=A/S-a\), we have \(\log S=q/b-(c/b)\log N\) and, reparameterising, \(S'=(q/b)-(c/b)N'\). The derivative of \(S'\) with respect to \(N'\) is then \(\partial S'/\partial N'=-c/b\), which is simply the elasticity \(E\) of \(S\) with respect to \(N\). This elasticity \(E=-c/b\) is the coefficient of economies of scale of total expenditure with respect to the number of members. Therefore,

  • \(E<1\): increasing economies of scale (expenditure \(S\) grows less than proportionally to the increase in the number of members \(N\)),
  • \(E=1\): constant economies of scale,
  • \(E>1\): decreasing economies of scale.

Once the elasticity \(E\) is known, the equivalence scale is

\[ U_{N+1}=U_{N}(1+E(\Delta N/N)) \tag{7.2} \]

where \(U_{N}\) is the poverty line for a family of \(N\) members. If \(E=0.667\) with \(U_{2}=986.35\), the poverty line for a household of three and of four members will be

7.4 Poverty indices: headcount, intensity and Sen

Entirely similar considerations apply to income levels. In particular, there are a number of useful indicators that help to analyse the poverty (of income or consumption) in a given area.

The headcount (diffusion) index, already computed (Table 7.2), is the ratio of the number of poor families (or persons) \(q\) to the total number of families \(n\)

\[ H=q/n \tag{7.3} \]

This index does not satisfy the monotonicity principle, according to which the index must increase if, other things being equal, the income (or consumption) of even a single poor individual falls. Nor does it satisfy the transfer principle, which requires the index to increase if income is transferred from a poor individual to another poor individual with higher income; moreover, it is insensitive to changes in the income distribution.

Another well-known index is the intensity (income-gap) index

\[ I=\sum_{i} g_{i}/qz \tag{7.4} \]

with \(i=1,...,q\), where \(z\) is the poverty line, \(g_{i}=z-y_{i}\) and \(y_{i}\) the income of the \(i\)-th poor individual. The index \(I\) satisfies the monotonicity principle, but it may fail to register changes in the number of poor individuals unless these come with changes in the total gap. Moreover \(I\), like \(H\), does not satisfy the transfer principle and is insensitive to changes in the income distribution.

Let us look at an example with made-up data.

  • R
  • Python
  • SAS
x <- c(3,4,6,7,8,9,12,15) # incomes
x <- sort(x)
k <- 10 # poverty line
xp <- x[x<k]
H <- sum(x<k)/length(x)
H
[1] 0.75
I <- sum(k-xp)/(k*length(xp))
I
[1] 0.3833333
x = np.sort(np.array([3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 12, 15]))   # incomes
k = 10                                               # poverty line
xp = x[x < k]
H = np.mean(x < k)
I = np.sum(k - xp) / (k * len(xp))
print(H, I)
0.75 0.38333333333333336
proc iml;
  x = {3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 12, 15};   /* incomes (column vector)  */
  call sort(x, 1);
  k = 10;                           /* poverty line */
  xp = x[loc(x < k)];
  H = mean(x < k);
  I = sum(k - xp) / (k*nrow(xp));
  print H I;
quit;

The index \(H\) ranges from 0 (no poor) to 1 (everyone poor), while the index \(I\) ranges from zero (no poor), or from a minimum close to 0 (incomes just below the poverty line), to 1 (incomes far below the poverty line).

Another important index is Sen’s index

\[ S=H\left\{1-[1-I][1-G]\right\} \tag{7.5} \]

where \(G\) is the Gini index computed on the income distribution of the poor; we will return to the index \(G\), and hence to Sen’s index, shortly. Sen’s index satisfies three axioms: the weighting axiom (the weight given in the index to each gap equals its rank in the non-decreasing ordering of the gaps); monotonic welfare (if one individual’s income is higher than another’s, the welfare of the former is also higher than that of the latter); normalised poverty value (if all the poor have the same income, the index equals the product of the headcount index \(H\) and the intensity index \(I\)). Sen’s index satisfies the monotonicity and transfer principles, is an increasing function of \(H\), \(I\) and \(G\), and does not depend on the income distribution among the non-poor. Finally, Sen’s index ranges from 0 (no poor) to 1 (incomes far below the poverty line).

7.5 Concentration: Lorenz curve and Gini index

The measurement of concentration, usually restricted to income (a typically transferable variable), is clearly linked to poverty analysis. For 4 individuals who own 100 euro in total, which split is fairer: 80, 10, 5 and 5, or 60, 35, 5 and 0? Apart from the two limiting cases, 100, 0, 0, 0 and 25, 25, 25, 25, the answer is not easy. Of course a distribution is fairer the less concentrated the phenomenon appears, which in this case must be a transferable variable.

Consider a set of \(n\) income earners, each with a certain income level, for a total of, say, \(d\). We order this distribution from the lowest to the highest income levels. Under equal distribution, i.e. no concentration, all individuals have the same share of income: the first 5% of individuals in the ordered distribution will have the first 5% of income, the first 10% of individuals will have the first 10% of income, and so on. We then define the following cumulative frequencies

\[ p_{i}=(1/n)+p_{i-1} \qquad \text{and} \qquad q_{i}=(\text{income of the }i\text{-th earner}/d)+q_{i-1} \tag{7.6} \]

Under equal distribution \(p_{i}=q_{i}\), otherwise \(p_{i}>q_{i}\). The Lorenz diagram shows the corresponding graph, with the cumulative values \(p_{i}\) on the horizontal axis and the cumulative values \(q_{i}\) on the vertical axis.

The greater the concentration, the larger the area between the line of equal distribution (\(p=q\), which on the graph is the bisector) and the so-called Lorenz curve, up to the limiting case in which it coincides with the area of the triangle with vertices \((0,0)\), \((1,0)\) and \((1,1)\). A concentration index \(R\) can thus be built as the ratio of the area between the line and the curve to its maximum value, the area of the whole triangle. The index \(R\), known as Gini’s concentration ratio, simplifies to the following formula if \(i/n\) has constant increments

\[ R=(2/(n-1))\sum_{i=1}^{n} (p_{i}-q_{i}) \tag{7.7} \]

or, more generally,

\[ R=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1} (p_{i}-q_{i})\Big/\sum_{i=1}^{n-1} p_{i} \tag{7.8} \]

with the following approximation for computing the Gini index

\[ G\cong (n-1)R/n \tag{7.9} \]

For our example we have

  • R
  • Python
  • SAS
n <- length(x)
p <- cumsum(rep(1/n,n))
q <- cumsum(x/sum(x))
RC <- sum(p-q)*2/(n-1)
GC <- (n-1)*RC/n
GC
[1] 0.2617188
n = len(x)
p = np.cumsum(np.repeat(1 / n, n))
q = np.cumsum(x / x.sum())
RC = np.sum(p - q) * 2 / (n - 1)
GC = (n - 1) * RC / n
print(GC)
0.26171875
proc iml;
  x = {3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 12, 15};
  n = nrow(x);
  p = cusum(j(n, 1, 1/n));
  q = cusum(x / sum(x));
  RC = sum(p - q) * 2/(n-1);
  GC = (n-1)*RC/n;
  print RC GC;
quit;

Gini’s concentration ratio ranges from 0 (no concentration) to 1 (maximum concentration). The Gini index, in its various formulations, is a very good index, since it satisfies some fundamental properties: scale invariance (the index is not affected by the unit of measurement: euro, millions, thousands, etc.), symmetry (the position of the values is irrelevant), sensitivity to transfers (if income is transferred from richer to poorer individuals, so that income concentration decreases, the index decreases).

Sen’s index is now easily obtained; note the equivalent formula used for the index \(G\) computed on the poor only.

  • Python
  • SAS
np <- length(xp)
Gp <- 2*sum(xp*1:np)/(np*sum(xp))-1-1/np; Gp
[1] 0.1936937

S
[1] 0.3770833
npov = len(xp)
Gp = 2 * np.sum(xp * np.arange(1, npov + 1)) / (npov * xp.sum()) - 1 - 1 / npov
S = H * (I + (1 - I) * Gp)
print(Gp, S)
0.1936936936936938 0.37708333333333344
proc iml;
  x = {3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 12, 15}; k = 10;
  xp = x[loc(x < k)];
  H = mean(x < k);
  I = sum(k - xp) / (k*nrow(xp));
  np = nrow(xp);
  Gp = 2*sum(xp # T(1:np)) / (np*sum(xp)) - 1 - 1/np;
  S = H*(I + (1-I)*Gp);
  print Gp S;
quit;

7.6 Other concentration indices

There are other important concentration indices. For example, Theil’s index

\[ T=\sum_{i=1}^{n} q'_{i}\log (nq'_{i}) \tag{7.10} \]

where \(q'_{i}\) is the non-cumulative income share. Theil’s index ranges from 0 (no concentration) to \(\log (n)\) (maximum concentration). Another useful index is Herfindahl’s

\[ Her=\sum_{i} (q'_{i})^{2} \tag{7.11} \]

which ranges from \(1/n\) with no concentration (\(y_{i}=y_{j}\ \forall\, i,j\)) to 1 in the case of maximum concentration.

  • R
  • Python
  • SAS
qq <- x/sum(x)
TH <- sum(qq*log(n*qq))
TH
[1] 0.1090448
Her <- sum(qq^2)
Her
[1] 0.1523438
qq = x / x.sum()
TH = np.sum(qq * np.log(n * qq))
Her = np.sum(qq ** 2)
print(TH, Her)
0.10904484133099582 0.15234375
proc iml;
  x = {3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 12, 15};
  n = nrow(x);
  qq = x / sum(x);
  TH  = sum(qq # log(n*qq));     /* Theil index */
  Her = ssq(qq);                 /* Herfindahl index */
  print TH Her;
quit;

Normative indices can also be used, which allow an a priori degree of inequality aversion to be set through a parameter \(r\). Among the best known is Atkinson’s index

\[ A= \begin{cases} 1-\dfrac{1}{m}\left(\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i} y_{i}^{1-r}\right)^{1/(1-r)} & \text{for } r\in [0,1) \\[2ex] 1-\dfrac{1}{m}\left(\prod_{i} y_{i}\right)^{1/n} & \text{for } r=1 \end{cases} \tag{7.12} \]

where \(m\) is the arithmetic mean of the incomes \(y_{i}\).

  • R
  • Python
  • SAS
r <- 0.5
Atk <- 1-(1/mean(x))*((1/n)*sum(x^(1-r)))^(1/(1-r))
Atk
[1] 0.05530581
Atk1 <- 1-(1/mean(x))*prod(x)^(1/n)
Atk1
[1] 0.1112299
r = 0.5
Atk = 1 - (1 / x.mean()) * (np.mean(x ** (1 - r))) ** (1 / (1 - r))
Atk1 = 1 - (1 / x.mean()) * np.prod(x) ** (1 / n)
print(Atk, Atk1)
0.055305809622023805 0.11122985986844014
proc iml;
  x = {3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 12, 15};
  n = nrow(x); r = 0.5;
  Atk  = 1 - (1/mean(x)) * (mean(x##(1-r)))##(1/(1-r));
  Atk1 = 1 - (1/mean(x)) * exp(mean(log(x)));   /* geometric mean */
  print Atk Atk1;
quit;

Atkinson’s index ranges from 0 (no concentration) to \(1-1/n\) in the case of maximum concentration with \(r=0.5\), or to 1 with a unit parameter \(r=1\).
