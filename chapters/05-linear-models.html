5 Specification of linear economic models

5.1 The multiple regression model and goodness-of-fit statistics

In earlier sections we have repeatedly used the regression model for trend analysis or for extrapolation for forecasting purposes. The extensive use we make of linear models, not only for time series, calls for a dedicated treatment: on the one hand, to address the questions left unanswered so far and, on the other, to present in a unified and general way the important issue of model specification and of the interpretation of the corresponding estimates.

The file abitazioni.csv contains the sale prices (prezzo), lot size (dimensione), number of bedrooms (letto), number of bathrooms (bagno) and number of storage rooms (ripostiglio) of 546 houses. The estimates show that all the variables are significant except the intercept, which in any case takes an implausible negative value. The other estimates are positive, indicating that an increase in the \(X\) leads to an increase in the house price \(Y\). In particular, if lot size is measured in square metres and price in dollars, we can say that, other things being equal (that is, holding the other explanatory variables fixed), a 1 m² increase in lot size corresponds to an increase in house price of about $5.43. Likewise, with the other explanatory variables held fixed, an extra bedroom raises the house price by $2,825 on average, although the explanatory power of this variable appears less important than that of lot size (its p-value is higher); similar considerations apply to the other estimates. The p-value can be read as the lowest significance level at which the null hypothesis is rejected, which here is the hypothesis that the individual coefficient is zero. In other words, it is very unlikely that lot size has no influence on the prices in our sample and that what we observe is due to chance alone (the p-value is 0.000), whereas the same statement is riskier for the number of bedrooms (the p-value is 0.020).

Computing the coefficient of determination

\[ R^{2}=rgss/tss \tag{5.2} \]

is quite straightforward, where

\[ rgss=\sum (\hat{Y}-\bar{Y})^{2} \quad , \quad tss=\sum (Y-\bar{Y})^{2} \tag{5.3} \]

and \(tss=rgss+ssr\), with \(ssr=\sum (Y-\hat{Y})^{2}\).

rgss <- sum((yc-ym)^2)
tss <- sum((y-ym)^2)

\(R^{2}\) measures the share of the total variance of \(Y\) that can be explained by \(X\) through the linear model; high values indicate a good fit of the model to the observed data.

When using a multiple regression model, however, the coefficient of determination should be adjusted to take into account the number of explanatory variables. Indeed, \(R^{2}\) tends to increase simply because variables are added, so the adjustment becomes necessary when comparing linear models with different numbers of explanatory variables. The adjusted \(R^{2}\) is

\[ R_{c}^{2}=1-[(1-R^{2})(n-1)/df] \tag{5.4} \]

The \(F\) statistic, by contrast, tests the significance of the regression as a whole, with null hypothesis \(\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=\beta_{3}=\beta_{4}=0\); it can also be computed from the relationship between \(F\) and \(R^{2}\).

As the p-values show, both the \(F\) statistic and the \(\beta\) estimates are significant, which implies observed test statistics inside the rejection regions of the null hypothesis.

Given the relationship between \(F\) and \(R^{2}\), we can also find a critical value for the coefficient of determination under the null hypothesis that \(R^{2}\) equals 0

\[ R_{t}^{2}=((k-1)F_{t})/((k-1)F_{t}+(n-k)) \tag{5.5} \]

where \(k\) is the number of parameters of the model, \(n\) the number of observations and \(F_{t}\) the critical value of the \(F\) statistic.

  • SAS

The coefficient of determination is beyond the critical value; so, at 95% confidence, the null hypothesis \(R^{2}=0\) is rejected.

Omitting relevant explanatory variables biases the estimator \(\beta\), and the bias is stronger the closer the relationship between the included and the excluded explanatory variables, since the omitted variables act through their correlation with those included in the model. The solution, however, is not to add as many variables as possible: there is a trade-off between adding variables and complicating the model, with the resulting difficulties of interpretation and sometimes of estimation. Rather, the effort should be to add the variables that the economic literature and the analyst’s experience indicate as most important, without losing sight of the advantages of a parsimonious model and accepting the risk of leaving out some important variable anyway; some will slip through regardless, if not through the analyst’s error then perhaps because the statistical information is unavailable.

The opposite risk to excluding important variables is including statistically irrelevant ones. An irrelevant variable in the model generally has a low, but rarely zero, correlation with the other included explanatory variables; however low, this correlation affects the variance of the estimators: it can be shown that including an irrelevant variable does not bias the estimators of the other explanatory variables but increases their variance, and therefore reduces the precision of the estimates.

5.4 Multicollinearity

Obviously, everything is correlated with everything. But when the correlation among explanatory variables is too high, the statistical problem of multicollinearity may arise. With multicollinearity the model cannot properly attribute statistical weight to the individual explanatory variables and, in extreme cases, it signals a strong dependence of \(Y\) on the \(X\), with a high \(F\) statistic and a high \(R^{2}\), but no statistical significance for the individual explanatory variables; that is, the empirical values of the \(t\) statistics are low and fall in the acceptance region of the null hypothesis: an obvious contradiction.

With strong collinearity, a first simple step is to drop the explanatory variables that create statistical redundancy. But what if the variables are indicated by economic theory and their exclusion is justified statistically but not economically? In these cases, which are in fact the majority, the solution is not simple. One could transform the variables, try replacing them with different indicators or combine them into a new variable to replace those causing redundancy, but here too one risks stripping the model of economic meaning.

The model modcor with both explanatory variables cannot attribute to them their proper role in explaining price, precisely because they are strongly correlated: both the estimated coefficient and its statistical significance are distorted.

A quick way to see whether the correlation among the explanatory variables is dangerously high is the statistic

\[ VIF_{j}=1/(1-R_{j}^{2}) \tag{5.6} \]

where \(R_{j}^{2}\) is the coefficient of determination of the model in which the \(j\)-th explanatory variable is regressed on the others. The VIF follows an empirical rule whereby, if \(VIF_{j}>10\) (corresponding to an \(R^{2}\) above 0.90), \(X_{j}\) is considered highly correlated with the remaining explanatory variables. Since the VIF ranges from 1 (no collinearity) to infinity, its reciprocal is sometimes preferred

\[ TOL=1/VIF \tag{5.7} \]

known as tolerance; it ranges from 0 (greatest collinearity) to 1 (least collinearity). For our example

Nevertheless, strong correlation among the explanatory variables (within certain limits) is not a problem if we are not specifically interested in the coefficient estimates but are looking for a model that fits the data reasonably well and provides a satisfactory short-term forecast.

5.7 Parameter restrictions and the F test

The \(F\) test is particularly useful for assessing specific restrictions on the parameters. Suppose we want to check whether the coefficients of dimensione and dimcli can be considered different only by chance. Then \(Y=\alpha +\beta_{1}X_{1}+\beta_{2}X_{2}+e\) is the extended model, while \(Y=\alpha +\beta_{1}(X_{1}+X_{2})+e\) is the restricted model to be tested (now there is a single \(\beta\)). Since

The \(F\) test is also useful for testing the structural stability of the coefficients of a model estimated over two different time periods, in two different areas or on two different groups of cases (for example, less and more affluent consumers). This particular test statistic is known as the Chow test

\[ \begin{gathered}F_{Chow}=\left((rsum-(usum_{a}+usum_{b}))/k\right)/ \\ \left((usum_{a}+usum_{b})/(n_{a}+n_{b}-2k)\right)\sim F_{k,(n_{a}+n_{b}-2k)}\end{gathered} \tag{5.9} \]

where \(k\) is the number of parameters, \(rsum\) is the residual sum of squares of the restricted model with structural stability (a single model for all observations), while \(usum_{a}\) and \(usum_{b}\) are the corresponding sums for the unrestricted models, estimated separately for the two groups (\(a\) and \(b\)) of observations.

5.13 Heteroscedasticity

When estimating linear models, a condition that is often not met is homoscedasticity, that is, the assumption of constant variance. The error variance may not be constant for various reasons, for example because of an incorrectly specified functional form, the presence of outliers, etc. With heteroscedasticity the estimators are unbiased and consistent but not efficient. One way of dealing with the problem is to use generalised least squares (GLS), weighting, for example, the observations in inverse proportion to the size of the variance (weighted least squares, WLS).

There are several techniques and tests for detecting heteroscedasticity; one of them is the Goldfeld-Quandt test, which assumes that the variance is positively related to a specific explanatory variable.

5.14 Autocorrelation of the residuals

Another common violation of the basic assumptions in estimating a model is autocorrelation of the residuals; it appears over time in time series, across space in cross-section data, or both in more complex situations. The last two cases call for a more detailed treatment that is beyond our scope, but we can give some examples of serial autocorrelation.

If the model can be written as \(Y_{t}=\alpha +\beta_{1}X_{1}+\beta_{2}X_{2}+u_{t}\), where \(u_{t}\) is not the classical white noise \(\varepsilon_{t}\) but a disturbance correlated with itself over time, then in the simplest case we can write

\[ u_{t}=\rho u_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t} \tag{5.11} \]

which is very reminiscent of a first-order autoregressive model, \(AR(1)\); \(\rho\) is called the first-order autocorrelation coefficient. Of course the dependence of the errors over time could follow a more complex structure with a higher lag, but in most cases the first-order assumption is satisfactory. If we used OLS with serial autocorrelation (as with heteroscedasticity), the estimators would be unbiased and consistent but not efficient.

  • \(4-d_{L}<d<4\): negative autocorrelation
  • \(d_{U}<d<4-d_{U}\): no autocorrelation
  • \(d_{L}\le d\le d_{U}\): inconclusive region
  • \(4-d_{U}\le d\le 4-d_{L}\): inconclusive region.

Note that the Durbin-Watson statistic is symmetric around \(d=2\). Moreover, its correct application requires strict conditions, including the absence of the lagged dependent variable among the explanatory variables.

Finally, note that in some cases strong autocorrelation, as already pointed out for heteroscedasticity, may result from model misspecification rather than from genuine serial dependence in the data.

5.15 Correcting for autocorrelation

Suppose now that \(\rho\) is known and equal to the correlation between \(\hat{u}_{t}\) and \(\hat{u}_{t-1}\). We estimate a transformed model which, while not eliminating serial autocorrelation entirely, at least reduces it and its consequences for the estimates. The procedure applies OLS to the transformed model, which is equivalent to GLS on the original model \(Y=f(X)+u\), that is, \((Y_{t}-\rho Y_{t-1})=\beta_{0}(1-\rho )+\beta_{1}(X_{t}-\rho X_{t-1})+\varepsilon_{t}\), where by construction \(\varepsilon_{t}=(u_{t}-\rho u_{t-1})\sim WN\).

def prais_winsten(y, x, rho):
    """Transform y and x with the given rho, keeping the first observation."""
    yy = np.r_[y[0] * np.sqrt(1 - rho ** 2), y[1:] - rho * y[:-1]]
    xx = np.r_[x[0] * np.sqrt(1 - rho ** 2), x[1:] - rho * x[:-1]]
    return yy, xx

5.16 Dynamic forecasting

An autoregressive form in the residuals of the model can be exploited for forecasting. By appropriate substitution we have \(Y_{t}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}X_{t}+\rho u_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}\) and therefore

\[ \hat{Y}_{t+1}=\hat{\beta}_{0}+\hat{\beta}_{1}X_{t+1}+\hat{\rho}\hat{u}_{t} \tag{5.14} \]

This is an example of dynamic forecasting, since we take into account the errors \(u\) of the previous period. The following commands compare, ex post, the static and dynamic forecasts at time \(t+1\) for the log-linear model of wages on productivity, estimated over 1959-1996, for 1997.

5.20 Information criteria: AIC and SIC

Comparing more complex models, especially when specified quite differently from one another, requires a more reliable statistic; this is the case of the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Schwarz Information Criterion (SIC).

With the usual meaning of the symbols, the first can be defined as

\[ \log AIC=(2k/n)+\log (rss/n) \tag{5.15} \]

and suggests choosing the model with the lowest value. For the second criterion we have

\[ \log SIC=(k/n)\log n+\log (rss/n) \tag{5.16} \]

and again the preferred model has the lowest value.

Let us work through an example with the previous models modA and modB.
