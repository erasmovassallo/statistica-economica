Preface

NoteAbout the online edition

This online edition is based on the book Statistica Economica con R (2018) and adds equivalent Python and SAS code alongside every R example. The R and Python code is run each time the site is rebuilt, so the output may differ in formatting details from that shown in the printed edition. The site is available in English and Italian.

This book grew out of the wish to reorganise the teaching materials and exercises handed out in my university courses and to make them easier for students to use.

This edition is a revised and updated version of a book first published in 2009. It contains many changes from the previous edition: new topics have been added and some content that is no longer current has been removed. The book also aims more firmly at a broad readership, with an exposition suited to any student approaching Economic Statistics, even with only a minimal background in statistics. In particular, its integration with the open-source software R, which in practice accompanies every topic with numerous examples, gives it a strongly applied character. The resulting approach is therefore rather original: the book is easy to read and avoids excessive methodological and technical detail. Moreover, the chapters have been designed so that they can be read without necessarily following the order in which they are presented.

The book is divided into 12 chapters. Chapter 1 covers some basic notions of R, introducing the software to readers with less computing experience. It describes the functions for reading data and the most important commands for manipulating objects. Chapter 2 sets out the main accounts of the National Accounts and works through some calculations for the aggregates considered most important. Chapter 3 deals with Index Numbers, with particular attention to price indices, and with some deflation techniques. R is used to compute series at constant prices and to examine the differences between valuations at current and at constant prices, as well as how base changes are carried out and what they imply. Chapter 4 describes time-series analysis in a classical framework, with a mainly, though not exclusively, macroeconomic slant. It shows how to decompose a series into its main trend, cycle and seasonal components using several techniques. The examples show how R can be used to smooth the data with moving averages of odd order, or of even order when the seasonal lag is even. The software is also used to compute more complex exponential smoothing functions for series with or without a seasonal period. In addition, a first look at the estimation of linear models makes it possible to identify trend, cycle and season through an appropriate model specification. The typical forms of autoregressive and distributed-lag models, widely used in economics, are introduced. This topic is treated in detail in the following chapter.

Chapter 5 therefore deals with the statistical, econometric and interpretive aspects of linear specification, with particular reference to its limits and to the precautions it requires. It addresses model misspecification through the omission of relevant variables and the inclusion of irrelevant ones, the presence of multicollinearity or of residual autocorrelation, and the lack of structural stability of the parameters or of homoscedasticity, to name just a few examples. A statistically reliable and robust model formulation, particularly for forecasting purposes, may require a specification that assumes a stochastic origin for the time series. In this respect, Chapter 6 follows the modern approach, assuming that a time series is generated by a stochastic process and can be represented by a model with an autoregressive and/or a moving-average component. Extensive use of data and exercises in R makes it possible to assess the descriptive and forecasting performance of these models.

Chapter 7 is devoted to measures of poverty and income distribution. The software is used to compute the best-known indices of poverty incidence and intensity; in addition, several exercises compute the Gini concentration ratio and index, the Theil and Sen indices, and others. Chapter 8 focuses on the concepts of production and productivity with reference to the production function and, in particular, to the Cobb-Douglas form, to which many exercises are devoted. Chapter 9 is devoted to measuring the statistical efficiency of production processes through parametric and non-parametric procedures. Although the latter require knowledge of constrained maximisation and minimisation problems, a simple exposition is offered to introduce the reader to a topic of real importance in evaluating the performance of public services. Chapter 10 covers the main Shewhart control charts, for attributes and for variables, which are used to monitor the evolution of a production process and detect possible anomalies. Once again, the topic is relevant to measuring and analysing process quality, with particular attention to public services.

Chapter 11 introduces particular autoregressive forms that focus on estimating the variance of financial time series. It presents autoregressive heteroscedastic models and their variants most widely used in analysing the characteristics and behaviour of financial markets. Economic time-series approaches rest essentially on a relationship of dependence along the time axis. It is intuitive, however, that data for one area are subject to a contagion effect and in turn affect data for neighbouring areas, so that their evolution is worth examining not only over time but also in space. This topic, which also matters for analysing and interpreting territorial disparities, is introduced in Chapter 12, together with further examples that briefly recall some multivariate techniques; a more detailed treatment of these subjects is beyond the scope of the book.

It should be stressed that the chapters need not be read in order, although, for obvious expository reasons, later sections do not repeat the descriptions of R functions given earlier in the book. The 12 chapters are complemented by an online section at http://www.unipa.it/persone/docenti/v/erasmo.vassallo/testo18a, with additional materials, further reading, data, scripts and anything else useful for getting the most out of the book.

Clearly, the topics and applications covered in the book provide a general, didactically simplified and non-exhaustive overview of the main subjects of Economic Statistics; it is equally true, however, that they cover most of the basic training needs of the discipline in the various courses (both bachelor’s and master’s) offered by Italian universities.

Hoping to have produced a useful book for students approaching Economic Statistics, given also its empirical and applied approach built around the increasingly popular R software, it only remains for the author to thank all those who will kindly point out errors, oversights and typos in the book.

Palermo, October 2018
